Exiger Overview

A redesign of an existing website overview experience, focused on improving clarity, information hierarchy, and consistency through research-informed design.

Role Product Designer
Focus UX/UI, Information Architecture, Design Systems
Project Website Overview Redesign
Product Supply chain risk overview

The redesigned Overview leads with a small set of summary metrics, then a more focused geographic view.

The Overview page was carrying too much of the product at once.

I looked critically at an existing Overview experience for a supply chain risk platform. The page was meant to orient people: to show what needed attention, where risk was concentrated, and how to start working. Instead, it presented a dense field of widgets that competed for equal attention.

The problem was not only visual. The page needed clearer organization, a stronger hierarchy, and a more intentional system for presenting information so the Overview could actually function as an overview.

The Problem

Evaluating the existing Overview made the core issue obvious: almost everything on the page was treated as equally important.

Widgets sat in a tight grid with similar scale, similar chrome, and similar visual weight. Empty or low-signal modules occupied the same space as critical alerts. The map was saturated with markers. Customization lived behind small, easy-to-miss controls. Related information was present, but the relationships between sections were hard to read at a glance.

The page asked users to assemble meaning themselves, rather than guiding them through it.

An evaluation of the existing Overview, organized around four recurring issues: control discoverability, customization without preview, map overload, and metric density.

Unclear hierarchy

Cards, charts, lists, and empty states were given similar visual weight, so the page never established what to look at first.

Competing content

Too many modules appeared at once. Individual insights were present, but they were difficult to isolate from the surrounding noise.

Inconsistent patterns

Customization, template switching, and widget selection used small or incomplete patterns that did not form a coherent system.

A page that was hard to read as a whole

Once populated, the original Overview became a wall of modules. Heatmaps, alert totals, activity lists, and empty financial cards all sat at similar scale. Key metrics were not consistently placed at the top of the experience. They had to be found.

That made the page difficult to understand at a glance, and even harder to narrate. There was no stable path from “what matters” to “where it is happening” to “what I should do next.”

The original map carried too many markers, filters, and neighboring widgets at once, reducing its usefulness as a focusing tool.

Research / Discovery

Evaluating where the Overview created friction

I audited the existing experience and studied how other products handle maps, templates, widgets, and dashboard density. The goal was not to copy those products. It was to understand which patterns actually help people make sense of a complex overview.

01

Information hierarchy

The original page did not establish a clear hierarchy between summary metrics, geographic context, and supporting modules. Everything arrived with similar emphasis, so the Overview never resolved into a primary story.

02

Comprehension

Users had to work harder than necessary to understand what the page was communicating. Dense marker fields, overlapping widgets, and small action controls made it difficult to know where to focus.

03

Consistency

Widget selection showed names without visual previews. Template and customize actions were easy to miss. The page presented an opportunity to replace one-off treatments with a more cohesive set of components.

04

Navigation / flow

The organization of the page could better guide people through the information: a role or view first, a small set of signals next, then geographic context, then supporting activity, rather than a simultaneous field of modules.

Looking at how other products create overview clarity

I compared approaches to maps, template switching, widget selection, and dashboard structure. The useful patterns were consistent: preview before commit, make the active view a first-class control, and keep dense data from flattening the hierarchy.

Research notes on how other products surface maps, templates, widget previews, and dashboard starting points.

The goal was not a more decorative page. It was a clearer system for communicating information.

Every design decision had to help someone understand the Overview faster: what matters, where it is, and how the page is meant to be used.

Design principles

Clarity

Make important information immediately understandable. Lead with a short set of signals instead of asking people to scan a wall of modules.

Hierarchy

Create a stronger visual and informational order so users know what to look at first: summary, geography, then supporting activity.

Consistency

Use reusable patterns like cards, views, filters, and customization so the Overview feels like a system rather than a collection of widgets.

Scannability

Structure content so people can understand the page without reading everything linearly. Large numbers, grouped markers, and clear labels do the work.

Cohesion

Unify the visual language across metrics, map, activity, and customization so the experience holds together as one overview, not several products stacked together.

Deciding what the map should do

The map was one of the most important, and most overloaded, parts of the original page. I explored whether it should sit as a background, a side widget, or a quieter contextual layer.

The direction that held up was not “more map.” It was a map that preserves geographic context without drowning the metrics around it: clustered markers, readable filters, and enough space for the rest of the Overview to breathe.

  • Background maps added context but increased cognitive load.
  • Side-widget maps protected the data, but risked making location feel secondary.
  • A quieter, clustered map kept geography visible without competing with summary information.

Widget selection was explored as a full preview grid, a descriptive list, and a list with hover preview, aiming to reduce trial-and-error during customization.

A system, not a set of screens

The redesign was built from reusable patterns: summary cards, view management, category filters, and a customization modal with a dedicated preview. The Overview is an arrangement of those parts, not a one-off layout.

Summary cards share a single structure: icon, label, and a dominant value. Color is reserved for meaning, not decoration.

The same card pattern can hold different metrics without changing how the page is read.

Customization with a preview

The original “Add Widgets” experience listed names and categories, but not what a widget would look like. That forced trial-and-error. The redesigned modal keeps browsing lightweight, with search, category, title, and a short description, then shows a live preview of the selected widget before it is added.

  • Typography: title, description, and preview value are clearly ranked.
  • Components: search, dropdown, selectable cards, and a primary Done action.
  • Hierarchy: choose on the left, confirm on the right.

Search and category filters make a large widget library browsable without turning the modal into another dense dashboard.

Views make template switching a first-class control, instead of hiding it in the header.

The redesigned Overview is meant to be read in layers.

A view, a few signals, a map, then supporting activity. The page still holds complexity. It just no longer presents all of it at the same time.

Default View, a short metric row, and a clustered map form the primary scan path.

Supporting modules like recent alerts, command centers, recently viewed, and recently updated sit below the primary scan, grouped instead of scattered.

Views as a first-class control

The original page hid template switching in a small header control. The redesign treats templates as part of the page language: a visible Default View, an edit affordance, and a menu of curated layouts for common roles.

That change is structural. It tells people the Overview is not one generic dashboard. It is a set of intentional arrangements of the same system.

Making the platform’s building blocks clearer

Templates, presets, and customization were supposed to be part of the platform. The work was not to invent them. It was to make them clearer, easier to find, and more complete as a system.

The empty state already pointed to templates and widgets. Those starting points needed to be clearer, and the populated page still lacked a readable hierarchy.

Template switching was part of the platform, but it lived in a compact header control that was easy to overlook.

Role-based templates were meant to give people a starting point. They needed a clearer place in the interface, not a buried modal.

Category filters were part of the intended customize flow. They needed a clearer pattern, including a preview of the widget itself.

Key improvements

The redesign did not add more information. It changed how the existing information is ordered, grouped, and presented.

01

Clearer hierarchy

Information was reorganized so the page can be understood at a glance: a few summary metrics first, then geography, then supporting activity.

02

Better content structure

Related information is grouped into a stable sequence instead of a simultaneous grid of equal cards.

03

Stronger design-system usage

Reusable cards, views, filters, and a preview-based customize pattern make the Overview feel like one system.

04

Improved scannability

Clustered map markers, large metric values, and clearer sectioning let people identify what matters without reading the page linearly.

05

More intentional visual design

The visual system supports the content instead of competing with it. Density is still available. It is no longer the default state of the Overview.

Reflection

This project was a reminder that overview pages fail quietly. They can contain the right data and still not communicate.

The work was less about inventing new screens and more about deciding what the page is for. Once that was clear, hierarchy, grouping, and component patterns became the actual design problem, not polish for its own sake.

I would still iterate on how views, customization, and the map share attention. But the takeaway is simple: thoughtful product design improves an existing experience by looking past visual finish and addressing structure, systems, and comprehension.
